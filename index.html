Programming Paradigms

SW7 · Autumn 2026 · Aalborg University Copenhagen

This course examines key design decisions and major differences between programming languages, focusing on characteristics such as state, control flow, process communication, data types and modularity.

These decisions shape everyday engineering: git branches instantly because it never overwrites data, allowing access to all previous states; WhatsApp serves hundreds of millions of users on message-passing concurrency; Android adopts Rust because most security vulnerabilities are memory errors that the language design rules out through its type system. These concepts, however, have a wider scope than any single technology or programming language that only make some design choices default. As AI assistants generate more of the code, recognizing the paradigm beneath an unfamiliar program becomes a core engineering skill. In this course, we will learn to identify and apply these design decisions through study, practice, and implementation.

Thirteen sessions, every Wednesday 12:30–16:15: a 2×45 min lecture, then a 2 h lab with homework. We already know Java, C#, Python and some C; we will read and write OCaml and other languages as the ideas require them.

Session titles are listed from the start. Topics, languages and lab material appear for each session as we reach it — later sessions may still change.

Exam format

Oral examination based on a project. MSc Software, 1st semester, 5 ECTS, external assessment, 7-point scale. Approved by exemption from the curriculum (case 2026-422-03081), in force from E26. Announced 16 September 2026.

In brief

Each student, individually or in a group, carries out a mini-project in which one or more design decisions studied in the course (state, control flow, concurrency and process communication, data types, modularity) are applied in code. The group submits a short report with code. The exam is an oral examination on the submitted material and on the course curriculum. The assessment is individual.

The mini-project

A small, self-contained piece of software together with a report that explains it. One of three options:

  • (a) Extension of the semester project. A delimited extension that applies course material to the group's own semester project, under the rules below that keep the two assessments apart.
  • (b) A topic from the suggested list, published on Moodle on 7 October (Session 6).
  • (c) A topic of the students' own choice, approved by the lecturer against the module's learning objectives. Proposals are sent to the lecturer by 28 October (Session 9).

Any programming language is allowed. The expected scope is about the work of two lab sessions per student, including the report. What counts is not size but that the design decisions are made deliberately, justified, and compared with alternatives.

Groups

  • Groups are voluntary. Students work in a group of two to five, or individually. Groups need not coincide with the semester project groups.
  • Groups, or the choice to work individually, and the topic are registered by 4 November (Session 10) through the form on Moodle. Students who have not registered by then are examined individually on a topic from the list.
  • Leaving a group. The same opportunities for group separation apply as in other exams. A student may leave a group at any time before the submission deadline by informing the lecturer and the study secretariat. Each part then submits its own report. Material produced jointly before the split may be used by both parts, and each report states the split and the shared material.
  • Special conditions and hardship cases are handled by the Study Board.

The report and the code

  • Report. At most 10 pages for a group and at most 5 pages for an individual student, excluding front page, references and appendices. PDF, in English. It describes the problem, the design decisions taken and the alternatives rejected, the implementation, and a short reflection on what the chosen paradigm gave and cost.
  • Code. A link to a repository with a tag or commit hash, or an archive uploaded with the report. The report says how to build and run it.
  • Joint work. The report is the group's joint work. It does not state who wrote which part (Examination Policies and Procedures, section 4.2). Individual assessment happens at the oral exam.
  • Aids. All aids are allowed in the written part, including generative AI, subject to the AAU rules on the use of generative AI: use is declared in the report, and reused text and code are referenced.
  • Submission in Digital Exam, by the deadline set by the study secretariat, about one week before the oral exams (early January 2027). The exact date is announced on Moodle and in Digital Exam.
  • Formal requirements. Report in PDF within the page limit, with names and student numbers on the front page, and the code available at the stated location. A report not submitted on time counts as absence and uses an examination attempt. A report that does not meet the formal requirements is registered as not assessed and uses an examination attempt.

Extensions of the semester project: rules against double assessment

Work may not be submitted for assessment twice. If the mini-project extends the semester project:

  1. The extension is a delimited, separately assessable piece of work: its own directory, branch or tag, and its own report.
  2. The report records the baseline: the repository and commit of the semester project on which the extension builds, and lists precisely what is new. Everything not listed as new is treated as cited work, not as the group's PP work.
  3. The semester project's own material is not resubmitted. Its code is referenced, not assessed; its report text is not reused.
  4. The mini-project report's text is not reused in the semester project report. The semester project report may refer to the extension as a cited source.
  5. If the PP group is not the whole semester project group, the report states this and cites the other members' code as the baseline.
  6. The group informs its semester project supervisor of the extension.

The mini-project is assessed only against the learning objectives of Programming Paradigms; the semester project is assessed only at the project exam.

The oral exam

Held in the January 2027 examination period with the examiner and an external examiner. Dates and times are published by the study secretariat.

  • Group. A joint presentation of the mini-project, up to 5 minutes per student and at most 20 minutes in total, then examination of about 10 minutes per student on the report, the code and the course curriculum. The exam takes the form of a conversation, and every student is expected to be able to explain any part of the submitted work.
  • Individual. A presentation of up to 10 minutes, then about 10 minutes of examination.
  • Aids at the oral exam: the submitted report and code. Students may run their code.
  • Assessment. Each student receives an individual grade on the 7-point scale, based on an overall assessment of the report and the oral performance against the module's learning objectives. Each student has the right to receive the grade and its reasoning without the other group members present. The group then receives joint feedback.
  • A student who has submitted the report but does not attend the oral exam has used an examination attempt and receives the lowest grade (Examination Policies and Procedures, section 1.3.1).

Re-examination

The re-examination follows the guidelines for re-examinations in project modules (Examination Policies and Procedures, appendix 4). It is an oral examination, in one of three ways, decided by the Study Board on the lecturer's recommendation: (a) on the basis of the original report; (b) on the basis of a report revised or extended by a deadline agreed with the lecturer; (c) on the basis of a new mini-project. After a failed exam the lecturer sends the student and the Study Board a short written statement within 8 days, with a recommendation of which way applies and guidance on what to improve. Make-up exams after documented illness take place according to (a). The re-examination is held with 4 weeks' notice, or sooner by agreement with the student.

Why this format

The course aims for students to identify and apply language design decisions through implementation. An oral exam on drawn questions tests understanding, but not the ability to apply these concepts in code. As AI assistants generate more of the code, applying and explaining these decisions is the core competence the course must build and assess. The oral exam on the students' own code is also the assessment least affected by generated or copied work: an explanation cannot be handed in.
