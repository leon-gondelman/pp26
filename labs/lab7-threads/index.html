Programming Paradigms · Lab 7 · session 7: threads and shared memory

The Race Hunt

The lecture's thesis: the steps of one thread interleave with the steps of every other, and a program is right only if it is right in every interleaving; a run can show a bug, and only an argument shows there is none. The lecture gave the primitives: threads, the lock, the atomic instruction, the monitor's wait, the join, the visible write. The monitor's wait is in the appendix of the slides of block 1, Condition synchronization: Dijkstra's bounded buffer, the monitor, example 5 and Mesa's monitors, which the lecture does not present; it is read before the bounded buffer and the barrier below. The warm-up builds four structures on them, in Java, each under a contract and guarded by a check, and studies on paper the two primitives that are not structures, the join and the visible write. The quest builds one program from the structures, a ray tracer, in parts whose failures show in the picture.

Three phrases the lab uses as names, from session 6: not at the same time, two threads are never both inside the part that changes shared data; not before, a thread does not act until another has acted; visibility, a write by one thread is seen by a read on another. The lock, the atomic instruction and the per-thread copy settle the first; the join, the barrier and the monitor's wait settle the second, and each of them settles the third as well: what was written before the join, the arrival, or the release of the monitor is seen after it. The volatile write settles the third alone: the word written, and everything written before it, are seen by the thread that reads the word, and nothing else; it forbids no overlap and makes no two fields change as one.

The warm-up is the lab: Counter, Workers and BoundedBuffer, with questions 6, 7, 8, 1 and 5 of the primitives' page between them, in the order below; what is not reached in the lab is finished at home. The extensions, over the week: Barrier, and the rest of the page. The quest's required path, parts 1, 2, 3 and 6, needs only Counter and Workers; the BoundedBuffer's use is its own check here and, after the lab, the mini-project's queue. Nothing is handed in: the lab collects nothing.

The lab needs the JDK of session 1. The structures are in warmup/, one file each, with their checks. A hole is marked TODO n and holds throw todo(n) until the code is written in its place; two holes hold a wrong line instead, said where they are. A check can show a bug and cannot prove its absence (rule 8 of the rules sheet): every check runs its structure under heavy contention, so that the bug of each given version showed on every run made while writing the lab; where a failure can be forced, it is forced. The rules are those of the rules sheet, cited by number. The primitives' page is warmup/primitives.pdf; its questions are also below, where the lab takes them up.

Warm-up

Part 1: The counter, three waysat the keyboard

One interface, four implementations: the racy one is given, and the exercise writes the three repairs of the lecture. Each is predicted before it is run.

From the lecture

n++ on a shared field is three steps, read, add, write, and another thread may run between any two of them (example 1; rule 3). Three repairs: the lock, one thread at a time inside the critical section (example 3); the atomic instruction, the three steps as one, fetch-and-add, or a compare-and-set loop in which the thread that lost the race retries (example 6); a count per thread, nothing shared while they run, the sum after the join (example 8).

The contract

add never loses an add: after every thread that adds has ended and been joined, get() is the number of adds. During the run, get() returns a value at least the number of adds that completed before the call began and at most the number that began before it returned; for the count per worker, the value is a sum of slots read one after another, not a snapshot. Three of the four implementations ignore the argument; the fourth, PerWorkerCounter, also offers get with a worker's index, that worker's own count. The racy implementation breaks both sentences: a reader may never see a write, and a plain long may be read half-written.

Exercise 1

Given the interface Counter, the racy RacyCounter, and the three classes LockedCounter, AtomicCounter and PerWorkerCounter with their holes, write the three: synchronized on both methods; an AtomicLong with incrementAndGet; an AtomicLongArray with one slot per worker, written by its owner only, summed by get(). The slot is atomic not against other writers, there are none, but so that a reader during the run sees the writes: a plain array would be exact after the join and a data race before it. Before each is run, the prediction goes as a comment above its class, in the line left for it: the exact total, less, more, or it depends on the run. The lecture's spin lock from AtomicBoolean.getAndSet is an optional fourth.

The check: java -cp out CounterCheck racy, then locked, atomic, perworker. Two halves, both given, read and not edited. First a schedule chosen by hand: two threads run the three steps of n++ on the check's own copy of the racy add, with a barrier between the read and the write, so that both read the same value and the second write loses the first, every time; the line shows the two reads, the two writes, and the one lost. Then the free run: twelve threads add many times each to the counter named, while a reader calls get() again and again. After the join the total must be the number of adds. Each read during the run is held to the contract's bounds: at least the adds completed before the call began, at most the adds begun before it returned, and never less than the read before; each adder marks its progress in a slot of its own, and the reader reads the slots around each get, so that a get left outside the lock is caught when it shows. The racy counter lost adds on every run made while writing the lab; a right total in one run proves nothing (rule 8).

Part 2: The visible write: questions 6, 7 and 8 of the primitives' pageon paper

The counter's racy run is a write that a reader never saw. Three questions on what makes a write visible, from the page; the answers go on a separate page, in the form each question gives.

From the lecture

The visible write. All reads and writes of volatile fields in a program fall in one order that every thread agrees on, consistent with each thread's own order; a write to a volatile field is seen by every later read of it in that order, on any thread, and everything written before that write is visible after that read. It does not make a read-modify-write one step, and does not make two fields change as one.

Question 6

Given the loop while (!stop) { } on a plain flag stop, written to true by another thread, say whether the loop may spin for ever and why the compiler is allowed to make it; then with stop declared volatile.

Answer form: may or may not, and the rule that allows it. The Workers check is this question run.

Question 7

Given a plain field data written by thread A before A sets the volatile flag ready to true, and a reader on thread B that sees ready true, say what B may read in data.

Answer form: the written value only, or anything.

Question 8

Given a volatile int n, two threads that each do n++ a thousand times, and the value of n printed after both are joined, say whether it is always 2000, and if not, the range it may fall in and why volatile does not make it 2000.

Answer form: yes, or a range and a sentence.

Part 3: Workers: start, join, stopat the keyboard

n threads over an indexed task, joined, and stopped through a flag. The join and the visible write of the page are what this class rests on.

From the lecture

A platform thread is made with new Thread(r), started with start() and awaited with join(); the join also makes everything the thread wrote visible to the thread that joins it (example 1). A flag or a result another thread must see goes through a volatile or an atomic, never a plain field (rule 9).

The contract

A Workers of n, then start with a task, runs the task once on each of n threads, with the thread's index; start on a Workers already started is an error, and a render that restarts makes a fresh Workers. join() returns only when every thread has ended, and everything they wrote is visible after it. stop() makes stopped() true on every worker from some point on; it ends nothing, and a task that does not ask will not stop. With the flag plain, the last sentence has no "from some point on": the language permits a worker never to see it.

Exercise 2

Given the class with its constructor, start, join, stop and stopped, the threads kept in an array, write the start, the join, and the stop, with the flag plain, as it is declared. The task reaches the flag through the object it closes over:

The check: java -cp out WorkersCheck, three lines. The first: each index runs once, on a thread of its own, distinct from the others', and every slot the workers wrote is there after the join. The second: a second start is an error. The third: workers whose loop body is empty but for the test of the flag; the check waits a while, calls stop, and at a deadline notes which workers stopped; then it ends the process, abandoning the rest. Its verdict is on the flag's declaration, which it reads from the class: a plain flag fails, whatever the run showed, because the language permits a worker never to see a plain write (rule 9); the run is reported beside the verdict as what was observed. Before the first run, then: the third line will fail, and it is not a bug in the stop just written. On this JDK, once the compiler has compiled the loop, it keeps the field in a register and the loop never ends, on every run made while writing the lab; on another machine every worker may stop, and that proves nothing (rule 8). Then volatile goes on the flag's declaration, TODO 4, and the check runs again: the language now guarantees that a read of the flag after the write, in the order of volatile accesses, sees it, and forbids the compiler to keep the field in a register; it states no bound on how soon the read happens. On the course's setup every worker stopped on every run: that is what was observed, beside what is guaranteed. This run is the second half of question 6.

Part 5: The bounded buffer as a monitorat the keyboard

Example 5 of the slides, in the appendix of block 1, Condition synchronization, which is read before this exercise; and the one-lock queue the mini-project starts from.

From the lecture

Every Java object is a monitor: a synchronized method holds its lock, wait() releases the lock and sleeps, notifyAll() wakes every thread waiting on that object. An object has one waiting set, so producers and consumers wait on the same bench (rule 5). Between the wake-up and the lock, another thread may have made the condition false again, and a wake-up with no cause is allowed too, so the condition is tested again after every wake-up: a loop does that and an if does not (rule 4).

The contract

Capacity K, one consumer. put blocks while K items are in; take blocks while none is. Items come out in the order they went in, each exactly once, and everything a producer wrote before its put of an item is visible to the thread whose take returns it. The end of work is a convention, not a method: the client puts a value it reserved as the end marker after the last item, and the consumer stops when take returns it; with many producers, the thread that joined them puts it. With the if, "each exactly once" fails; with notify, "blocks while" may become "blocks for ever".

Exercise 3

Given the ring of slots under the object's lock, put and take written with each wait checked once, and the convention of the end marker in the contract, write the two waits in a loop with notifyAll (TODO 1, TODO 2). Nothing else is a hole: the end marker is a value the client reserves, BufferCheck.END in the check and Frame.END in the quest, and the consumer loop that stops on it is the check's and is given. The check runs first as given, with the ifs, and its report is read; then with the loops.

The check: java -cp out BufferCheck: many producers against one consumer that pauses now and then, so that producers queue at the full buffer and one wake-up lets them all through the if; the main thread joins the producers and then puts the end marker, which is question 1 again. The check makes each put while holding the buffer's own lock, so that it can write down the order the items went in as it happens (a wait inside put releases the lock, so the producers still queue), and the consumer must take them in that order, each once. With the if, items never arrive, and the line names the wait. With one consumer, the if of take fails only on a wake-up with no cause, which the language allows and a run seldom shows; the lecture's example 5, with two consumers, showed it on every run. notifyAll does not remove the loop; the loop is what makes the extra wake-ups harmless. Then, as an experiment: change notifyAll to notify in both methods, run, and change it back. The run may stop, when a wake-up the consumer needed is delivered to a producer that can do nothing with it and everyone waits; the check reports it at a deadline, with the threads that wait, and says that a run that did not stop proves nothing (rule 8).

Part 6: The barrierextensionat the keyboard

Not before over a whole phase, built from the monitor: arrivals counted under the lock, the last runs an action and wakes the rest. The quest's video, part 5, needs it.

The contract

A Barrier of a count of parties and an action, the action allowed to throw InterruptedException. await() returns only after all parties have called it in this round, the action has run once, by the last arrival, before anyone is released, and everything any party wrote before its await, and everything the action wrote, is visible to every party after. The round ends when the last arrives; the next begins at once, and a party that returns cannot pass the next await until the others have arrived again. If the action throws, or a waiting party is interrupted, the barrier is broken: every party waiting and every later arrival leaves await with a BrokenBarrierException, and the barrier stays broken; that is CyclicBarrier's rule, and the quest's video ends its workers on it.

Exercise 4

Given the count of parties, the action, and the empty await(), write it, in two steps. First for one round: arrivals counted under the lock, every arrival but the last waits, the last runs the action, resets the count and wakes the rest. Then make it reusable with a round number: a waiter waits while the round it arrived in is still the current one. Without the round number two things break: the reset of the count makes a slow waiter's condition true again and it waits for ever; and a fast party arriving again before the slow ones have left corrupts the count. A waiting party that is interrupted, and an action that throws, break the barrier, as the contract says.

The check: java -cp out BarrierCheck, two lines. The first sends a few parties through many rounds as fast as they can, with an action that counts the rounds; a party that passes before the others have arrived is recorded, the action must have run once per round, by the last arrival, and a run that has not ended at a deadline is reported with the parties that wait. The second interrupts one waiting party: it leaves with InterruptedException, the other with BrokenBarrierException, and so does a later arrival.

Quest

The quest is one program, a ray tracer in Java, given whole and sequential: it reads a scene from a text file, spheres, cubes, a floor, lights, a camera, and writes a PNG, with shadows and mirror reflections. The quest makes it multi-threaded with the structures of the warm-up, in six parts, each a need the picture makes felt, two or three holes, and a payoff seen on screen. The holes are uses, not mechanisms: which structure goes where, in what order, with what end marker or restart, and the sentence that says why that structure and not another. By the end the program renders in parallel with a live bar, balances its work, takes a new camera from the keyboard while it renders, and, as extensions, samples the picture many times for soft shadows and renders a video of a turning cube with its reflection.

Parts 1, 2, 3 and 6 are the path, taken by every student in that order; parts 4 and 5 are extensions, over the week. Parts 1, 2, 3 and 6 use Counter and Workers only; part 5 is the one that needs the Barrier and the BoundedBuffer. Part 4: The film and the fold (extension)

The need: Jagged edges and hard shadows. Many samples per pixel, jittered, with the lights given a size, give smooth edges and soft shadows; and with lights brighter than 1 the picture needs tone mapping, which needs the whole picture's brightest value before any pixel is written to eight bits.

Given the design, stated here: rendering is progressive, the outer loop is over sample passes, and each pass hands out the tiles anew through the dispenser, so that the samples of one pixel come from different workers in different passes and the preview after each pass shows the noise clearing. Because of that, one shared film would be written by every worker; the film is therefore one per worker, Film.java, in fixed point: each sample's channel is capped at a radiance of 16, rounded to the nearest 1/4 096, and added as an integer at most 65 536 into one int per channel per pixel; with the sample count capped at 32 767 the sum stays below 2³¹, so no sum overflows, and the order of adding cannot change it. Given ToneMap.java, wrong in the way that shows: each band of the merged film is mapped by its own maximum, no fold. Given Rng.of(x, y, s), the generator of sample s of pixel (x, y).

Part 5: The video (extension)

The need: One cube turns, and its reflection in the mirror cube must turn with it.

Given Video.java, at one sample per pixel, with its architecture written at the top of the file: the workers live for the whole video; a frame is one buffer, its pixels owned by tiles as in part 3; the scene of a frame is an immutable value, Scene.at(k), held in one field that every worker reads and only the barrier's action writes, and so are the current frame and the dispenser. For frame k, each worker renders its tiles into the frame, then arrives at the barrier; the action, run by the last arrival before anyone is released, puts the finished frame into the buffer to the encoder and installs the next frame, or the end marker after the last; every worker leaves the barrier and starts frame k + 1. The encoder is the main thread: it takes frames until the end marker, writes each, and then joins the workers. Two happens-before chains carry a frame: the barrier publishes every worker's tiles to the last arrival, and the buffer's put and take publish the frame from the last arrival to the encoder; the barrier alone establishes nothing with the encoder, which is not a party. The action's put may block on a full buffer while the others wait at the barrier, which is harmless.

Given, as the program first runs: no barrier; each worker that finishes its tiles of frame k moves the scene on and begins frame k + 1 on its own, and the frame goes to the encoder when a plain count says everyone has finished. Write: the barrier at the end of each worker's frame, its action nextFrame; the hand-over's end, Frame.END into the buffer after the last frame, and the encoder's loop, take until the end marker; and, in place of code, the answer to this: why do the frames reach the encoder in order here without any parking, and in which design, frames rendered in parallel as a render farm does, would parking by frame number be needed?

  • hole 12: barrier.await() at the end of each worker's frame.
  • hole 13: the end marker after the last frame, and the encoder's loop until it.
  • the question: why in order, and when parking would be needed.

The check: the frames come in order and each equals its sequential frame; a run that never ends is reported at a deadline. Failure: frame 17 holds tiles from two scenes: what moved the scene before every worker had finished the frame?

Gives: The barrier in its place, and what happens without it: a frame torn in two rotations (teacher/pictures/p5-torn-frame.png), since a fast worker begins frame k + 1 on the moved scene while a slow one still renders frame k; shared state changed between phases, the scene, the frame and the dispenser, by one thread, inside the barrier, which is the part's lesson; the buffer in its place, with the waits in a loop and everyone woken (rules 4 and 5), and the hang when the end marker is forgotten; and ordering as a concern of its own, settled here by the phase structure and elsewhere by a sequence number.

The last question, and what comes next

For every piece of shared state in the program, the preview's read included, say which of the three ways protects it: ownership, the lock or the atomic word, a copy per thread; and for every hand-over between threads or phases, which mechanism orders it: a join, a barrier, a monitor's wait, a flag another thread can see; and why that one. The sentence holes are this question asked part by part; the answers stay in the student's files.
